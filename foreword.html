The Porch Foreword

Foreword

to the present edition

It is now some years since there came into the editor's keeping a sheaf of papers, water-stained and bound with a butcher's string, purporting to set down the whole art of the story-teller. Of their author nothing certain is known; of their argument, a great deal may yet be recovered, and it is to that end the following pages are offered.

That the manuscript is imperfect no honest editor would deny.The leaves were found, the bookseller assured me, beneath the floor of a let room in ——shire; a claim I record without endorsing. Whole gatherings are wanting between what we have here numbered the third and fourth discourses, and in not a few places the ink has fled the page altogether, leaving the sense to be guessed at from the shape of the wound.

In restoring the text the editor has held to a single rule:Conjectural readings are set within square brackets throughout. to emend only where the sense fails outright, and to mark every such intrusion plainly, that the reader may quarrel with him at his leisure. Better an honest gap, he has supposed, than a seamless invention passed off as the hand of another.

The author's purpose is nowhere better declared than in the opening of the first discourse, which runs thus:

Of all the arts, that of the maker of tales is reckoned the easiest, for every child possesses it, and the meanest gossip practises it daily; and it is for this very reason the hardest to do well, since a thing everywhere attempted is everywhere found wanting.

The First Discourse

It would be an idle modesty to pretend the work is without the faults of its age.He reserves a particular severity for those who, in his own phrase, "mistake the heaping of incident for the building of a tale." The author is fond of the lash, and falls now and again into a moralizing vein that the modern reader will find wearisome; he is certain, as only the dead can be certain, of matters we have learned to leave in doubt.

And yet the substance holds.The five divisions of the present edition — the Sentence, the Scene, the Person, the Plot, and the Revision — are the editor's, not the author's, though they follow his order faithfully. Strip away the periwig and the thunder, and what remains is a plain and serviceable account of how a sentence is made to carry its freight, how a scene is got upon its feet, and how a personage is brought to want something and to suffer for the wanting. These are not the fashions of a season; they are the joinery of the trade, and they do not alter.

The book is therefore given to the press as what it is: a fragment, but a living one, and more instructive in its ruin than many a whole and tidy treatise. If it should send a single reader back to his own page with a sharper eye, the labour of its recovery will have been repaid.

— The Editor, Michaelmas, 18—.
